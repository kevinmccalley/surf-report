What Makes a Great Longboard Wave

A longboard wave is not simply a small wave. The best longboard breaks share several characteristics: enough length to allow footwork along the board; a shape that stays open rather than sectioning and closing; enough push to generate speed without requiring the surfer to actively pump and generate their own momentum; and, ideally, a peeling quality that makes the nose ride — walking to the front of the board and hanging toes over the tip — possible rather than suicidal.

Powerful slabs and heavy shore-break barrels are not on this list. Long, rolling, soul-feeding point breaks and mellow beach breaks are.

1. First Point Malibu — Los Angeles, California

First Point Malibu is the spiritual home of longboarding. The right-hand point break at Malibu — the Malibu Colony point, just below Pacific Coast Highway — was where the modern surfboard was first developed in its slender, manoeuvrable form, and where the style of surfing we now call classical longboarding was born in the 1950s and 1960s. The wave peels along the point on south and southwest swells, producing a long, rolling right-hander that opens up for nose-riding on the inside section.

The wave is not powerful or hollow. It rewards timing, footwork, and positioning over athleticism. A well-executed cross-step to the nose at Malibu, toes hanging off the tip as the wave wraps around the point, is one of the defining images of surfing. It is also, in summer, one of the most crowded lineups in the world — hundreds of surfers on a good south swell.

Best season: June through September on south and southwest swells. The wave is effectively flat in winter when the Channel Islands shadow the point from north swells.

2. Noosa Heads — Queensland, Australia

Noosa has its own dedicated WSL Longboard Pro event each spring, and it is no coincidence. The points at Noosa — Tea Tree Bay, Granite Bay, and National Park Point, stepping north along the headland — are right-hand point breaks that receive south and southeast swells wrapping around the headland and produce long, perfectly shaped right-handers that are among the best longboard waves in the world.

The points at Noosa are not powerful. They are peeling, consistent, and forgiving — the kind of wave where a five-minute ride is entirely possible and a nose ride feels effortless because the wave holds its shape for so long. The headland is inside a national park, which limits development and keeps the setting extraordinarily beautiful. The town of Noosa Heads behind the headland is one of the most pleasant resort towns in Australia.

Best season: March through August on south and southeast swells. Offshore winds are most reliable in the early morning in autumn and winter. Water temperature: 20–24°C (68–75°F) in winter — a light wetsuit is comfortable but not essential.

3. Waikiki — Honolulu, Oahu, Hawaii

Waikiki is where it all began. The beach at Waikiki was where Hawaiian royalty — ali'i — rode the surf for centuries before Western contact, and where Duke Kahanamoku demonstrated surfing to the modern world in the early twentieth century and sparked the global spread of the sport. The gentle, rolling waves at Waikiki — protected from the open ocean by a shallow reef and Kapahulu Groin — are as close to perfect for longboarding as waves get.

The surf is mellow, consistent, and warm. The rides are long. The setting — the Diamond Head crater, the hotel skyline of Honolulu, the blue Pacific water — is iconic. Canoe surfing, stand-up paddleboarding, and classical longboarding all share the lineup at Waikiki, which is the most socially relaxed surf environment in the world. This is not high-performance surfing. It is pure joy in the water.

Best season: Year-round, with the most consistent and best-shaped waves arriving from south swells in summer (May–September). Wave height is gentle year-round — 0.5–1.5m (1.5–5ft) is the norm. Water temperature: 26–29°C (79–84°F) year-round — boardshorts always.

4. San Onofre — San Diego County, California

San Onofre State Beach is the most community-oriented surf spot in California. The beach — inside a state park adjacent to the San Onofre nuclear power plant — holds a mellow, right-hand point break that on a south swell produces long, rolling waves ideally suited to longboards, bellyboards, and any craft that benefits from a forgiving, peeling wave rather than a critical, hollow one.

The culture at San Onofre is warm and welcoming in a way that is unusual at well-known California breaks. Surf clubs with decades of history, vintage boards, board shorts rather than technical surf gear, and a social atmosphere that prioritises fun over performance define the lineup. The beach is also one of the few in Southern California where dogs are permitted, adding to its relaxed character.

Best season: June through September on south swells. The wave also handles early northwest groundswells in October and November.

5. Scorpion Bay (San Juanico) — Baja California Sur, Mexico

Scorpion Bay's multi-point right-hand wave system is tailor-made for longboarding. The south-facing bay receives south and southwest swells that wrap through the bay in sequence, and the resulting waves are long, forgiving, and perfectly shaped for cross-stepping, nose-riding, and the unhurried style of surfing that a board of nine feet or more allows.

On the best days — long-period south swell, low tide, light offshore — the points link together and a single ride can last three to four minutes. The village of San Juanico is small and quiet. Camping on the beach is the traditional way to experience it. The combination of the waves, the desert landscape, the fishing community, and the absence of any resort development makes Scorpion Bay one of the most authentic surf experiences available on the Pacific coast of North America.

Best season: May through October on south and southwest swells of 14+ second period.

6. C-Street (California Street) — Ventura, California

C-Street is a long, mellow right-hand point break in Ventura that has been a staple of the California longboarding scene for generations. The wave wraps around a rocky point and, on a northwest groundswell, produces walls that peel for several hundred metres with enough consistency and shape to reward footwork and classic longboard technique.

The wave is more forgiving than Rincon to the north and less crowded than Malibu to the south. It handles a wide range of skill levels — beginners find the inside sections manageable, while experienced longboarders can work the outer sections and find nose-riding pockets on the right wave. Ventura itself is an unpretentious surf town with a genuine local culture.

Best season: October through March on northwest groundswells.

7. Côte des Basques — Biarritz, France

Biarritz has been a surf destination since the 1950s — one of the first European cities where surfing took hold — and the Côte des Basques beach, just south of the city centre, is the wave that defines its longboard heritage. The break is a gently shelving beach break that, on a mid-sized southwest swell, produces rolling, slow-breaking waves that are ideally suited to classical longboard technique.

The wave is not powerful. On larger swells it can produce more critical conditions, but at its typical 0.5–1.5m (2–5ft), the Côte des Basques is a place for footwork, cross-stepping, and the French style of longboarding that has been developing here since the Biarritz Surf Festival began in 1953. The city itself — elegant Belle Époque architecture, extraordinary Basque cuisine, the Atlantic promenade — is one of the most civilised places to base a surf trip in Europe.

Best season: April through June and September through November for the best combination of swell, warmer water, and moderate crowds.

8. Rincon — Ventura/Santa Barbara, California

Rincon earns a place on this list because its long, rolling walls — described in the rights article for their quality at full size — also produce exceptional longboard conditions on smaller northwest groundswells. At 0.5–1.2m (2–4ft), the Cove section at Rincon is a rolling, peeling right-hander that allows extended footwork and nose-riding in the same way Malibu does but with longer walls and a more dramatic point break setting.

The key is size: Rincon at 1.5m+ (5ft+) is primarily a shortboard and mid-length wave. At smaller sizes it becomes a long, forgiving longboard playground. The cobblestone point, the winter light in the Channel, and the view north toward Santa Barbara make it one of the most beautiful surf settings in California regardless of how you ride it.

Best season: October through March on northwest groundswells. Shadowed by the Channel Islands from south swells — flat in summer.

9. Playa Guiones — Nosara, Guanacaste, Costa Rica

Playa Guiones is one of the most consistent waves in Central America — a wide, gently curving beach break that receives south swells with minimal obstruction and produces rolling, workable peaks across the full width of the beach. The wave is forgiving enough for beginners but has enough push and length for experienced longboarders to find the footwork zones that the style requires.

The Nosara area has become one of the most popular yoga and wellness destinations in the world alongside its surf culture, which has created an interesting demographic: the beach on a south swell morning is a mix of serious surfers, first-timers on rental boards, and every level in between. The town is deliberately low-key — no large hotels, limited development — and the surf schools operating here have been running well-regarded beginner and intermediate programs for years.

Best season: May through October on south swells. Water temperature: 27–29°C (81–84°F) — boardshorts year-round.

10. La Herradura — Lima, Peru

La Herradura (The Horseshoe) is a horseshoe-shaped bay in the Chorrillos district of Lima — one of the only surfable breaks directly accessible from South America's largest city. The bay faces southwest and receives south swells that wrap into the horseshoe and produce a long, rolling left-hand point break with enough shape and length to allow genuine longboard surfing.

The wave is not particularly powerful — the bay geometry slows and organises the swell, which is exactly what makes it work for longboarding. The water is cold year-round due to the Humboldt Current upwelling: 16–20°C (61–68°F) requiring a wetsuit even in summer. The experience of surfing a quality wave within view of a megacity of twelve million people — Lima's residential districts visible on the cliffs above — is unique.

Best season: March through October on south and southwest swells. Best: May through August.

11. Tofino — Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Canada

The wild Pacific coast of Vancouver Island is not an obvious longboard destination, but the beaches around Tofino — particularly Cox Bay and Long Beach in Pacific Rim National Park — produce rolling, consistent beach break waves that are both ideal for longboarding and set within one of the most spectacular natural environments on earth. Old-growth rainforest meets a wide, grey-sand beach; bald eagles circle overhead; bears occasionally visit the shoreline.

The surfing culture in Tofino is warm, community-oriented, and deliberately unhurried. Surf schools here have introduced generations of Canadians to the sport, and the established local surfer community rides everything from SUPs and longboards to performance shortboards in water that demands a thick wetsuit year-round.

Best season: September through November for the transition swell season — surf is consistent, conditions are often clean, and the autumn light on the temperate rainforest is extraordinary. Water temperature: 10–16°C (50–61°F) — a 4/3 or 5/4 wetsuit is essential.
