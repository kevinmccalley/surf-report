Why Morocco Has Become a Surf Destination

A decade ago, Morocco was a secret. Today the village of Taghazout, perched on the Atlantic coast roughly 80 kilometres north of Agadir, is one of the most visited surf destinations in the world. The reasons are straightforward: reliable, long-period Atlantic swell, consistent offshore winds driven by the trade wind system, a string of world-class right-hand point breaks stacked within a few kilometres of each other, warm sunshine even in winter, and a cost of living that makes extended trips accessible.

Understanding the seasons — which months bring the best swell, the best conditions, and the best crowds — is essential to planning a trip that delivers on its promise.

The Swell Window

Morocco's Atlantic-facing coast receives swell primarily from the North Atlantic storm track. Between October and April, a succession of low-pressure systems move across the North Atlantic, generating northwest swells that travel south and hit Morocco's coast on a consistent basis.

The key quality of Moroccan surf is not just size — it is the way Atlantic swell interacts with the long, rocky point breaks around Taghazout. These points are angled almost perfectly to receive northwest swells, which wrap around the headlands and produce the long, peeling right-hand walls that put Morocco on the global surf map. At spots like Anchor Point, a single wave can run for 400 metres or more.

Month by Month

October – November (Opening of Season)

The season opens in October as the first significant North Atlantic swells begin arriving. Water temperature is still warm (around 22°C / 72°F), crowds are building but not yet at peak, and the swell is typically consistent in the 1.5–2.5 m range. November adds frequency and size. This is many experienced travellers' favourite window — the combination of reliable surf, manageable crowds, and warm conditions is hard to beat.

December – January (Peak Season)

December and January bring the most consistent and powerful swells of the year. North Atlantic storm systems are at full strength, and long-period groundswells — often 14 to 18 seconds — arrive with regularity. Anchor Point can be 6–8 feet face height with textbook walls. The trade wind system keeps winds offshore during most mornings, producing the glassy conditions that show Morocco's point breaks at their absolute best. This is also peak tourist season — accommodation fills quickly and the lineup at the most famous spots is crowded.

February – March (Consistent with Thinning Crowds)

February is almost as consistent as December and January for swell, but the crowds begin to thin slightly as many winter travellers rotate through. Water is still relatively warm (around 19–20°C / 66–68°F). March can be excellent — strong swells still arriving, even lighter crowds, and the added benefit of longer days. The best overall value for experienced surfers.

April – May (Transition)

April marks the transition out of peak season. Swells become less frequent but can still be significant when they arrive. Water begins warming again. Many surf camps and schools are fully operational. For beginners and intermediates, April and May are ideal: enough swell to learn and progress, smaller crowds, warm sunshine, and the entire infrastructure of the Taghazout surf industry at your service.

June – September (Summer)

Summer in Morocco is hot, dry, and largely flat for surf. The North Atlantic storm track retreats northward and consistent swells rarely reach the Moroccan coast. What arrives is usually small, short-period wind swell. Some swell can arrive in September as the season begins its reset. Summer is best approached as a cultural experience rather than a surf trip, unless you are a beginner looking for mellow conditions or surfing further south toward the Canaries.

The Spots Around Taghazout

Anchor Point — The crown jewel of Moroccan surfing. A long, mechanical right-hand point break that produces one of the longest waves in the Atlantic when the swell is right. Best on northwest swells of 1.5–3 m at 13+ seconds. At its best it is a world-class wave; at its worst it is a crowd of 100 surfers sharing inconsistent sets. Worth it regardless.

Hash Point — Immediately north of Anchor Point, named for the herb sold locally for generations. A shorter, punchier right that breaks over a mix of rock and sand. Works on smaller swells than Anchor and is often less crowded. A good option when Anchor is overwhelmed.

Killer Point — A long, powerful point break south of Taghazout that needs a larger swell to show its best — typically 2 m or above. When it fires it produces long, heavy right-handers that rival Anchor Point in quality. Less consistent, but one of the best days in Morocco when it works.

La Source — A softer, more forgiving right-hander between Killer Point and Taghazout village. Works across a range of swell sizes and is one of the better options for intermediate surfers who want to experience the point break magic without committing to Anchor or Killer.

Beyond Taghazout: Safi

Safi — Located further north on the Moroccan Atlantic coast, Safi is a powerful right-hand point break that receives northwest swells with minimal protection. It can produce fast, heavy waves on the right swell — genuinely big-wave territory on the largest days. Less visited than Taghazout, rougher in character, and rewarding for experienced surfers who make the effort.

Practical Notes

Water temperature in Morocco ranges from around 17°C (63°F) in February to 23°C (73°F) in October. A 3/2 wetsuit covers the entire season for most surfers; a 4/3 is more comfortable in January and February.

Board-wise, Morocco's points reward a slightly longer, more down-the-line board than a standard performance shortboard — a mid-length or a shortboard with extra foam under the chest handles the long walls at Anchor Point and Killer Point better than a thin, tightly-rockered board built for punchy beach breaks. A step-up is worth packing for the bigger December–January swells. The points break over rock and urchin-covered reef shelves, so reef booties are cheap insurance, and a slightly longer leash than usual helps on the longer rides.

The offshore wind driven by the Canary Islands High typically blows in the morning before dying or shifting onshore in the afternoon. This makes morning sessions almost always better than afternoon sessions — a rhythm that suits surfers and the café culture of Taghazout in equal measure.

Check current conditions at Anchor Point or Killer Point before planning any session.
