Why Indonesia

No country on earth delivers more world-class surf per square kilometre than Indonesia. The archipelago stretches over 5,000 kilometres from Sumatra in the west to Papua in the east, and its southern coastline — from Java through Bali, Lombok, Sumbawa, and beyond — faces the open Indian Ocean almost directly. The Southern Ocean swell machine, firing below 40°S latitude, sends long-period swells northward for thousands of kilometres before they arrive at Indonesian reefs with tremendous precision and power.

The result is an almost implausible density of great waves: multiple world-class breaks within walking distance of each other on Bali's Bukit Peninsula alone; the legendary left-hand barrel of Desert Point on Lombok; G-Land in East Java, one of the longest left-hand point breaks on the planet; and the Mentawai Islands off Sumatra's west coast, which many professional surfers regard as the most consistent surf destination in the world.

Indonesia is also a place of extraordinary cultural depth. Bali's Hindu traditions — entirely distinct from the Muslim majority that characterises the rest of Indonesia — create one of the most visually and spiritually rich environments you will encounter anywhere. The people are genuinely warm. The food is exceptional. The cost of living, once you're there, is low.

This is not a one-trip destination. Most surfers who go once go back.

The Swell Window

Peak Season: May through October

The dominant swell window for nearly all of Indonesia's major surf breaks runs from approximately May through October, driven by the southeast trade winds that blow across the southern Indian Ocean during the southern hemisphere's autumn and winter months. These winds generate long-period swells — commonly 12 to 18 seconds — that travel northward and arrive at Indonesian reefs as clean, powerful groundswell.

The southeast trades also produce offshore or cross-shore winds at most Bali breaks during the morning hours, keeping conditions groomed before the afternoon sea breeze fills in. This combination of consistent swell and favourable morning winds is why June, July, and August are the busiest months at every surf spot in Bali and across the southern islands.

Water temperature throughout the season holds between 26–29°C (79–84°F) — board shorts and a rash guard are the standard kit for most surfers, though a thin 2/2 spring suit can be useful for long dawn patrol sessions or at exposed offshore spots.

Wet Season: November through April

The northwest monsoon arrives in late October or November, reversing the wind direction and bringing heavy rain, onshore winds, and significantly more chaotic conditions across Bali's south-facing breaks. Surf doesn't disappear entirely — swells still arrive — but the groomed morning glass of the dry season is replaced by textured, windblown faces much of the time. Crowds thin considerably, prices drop, and some experienced travellers actively seek out the wet season for its quieter lineups.

A few important caveats: the Mentawai Islands receive swell year-round and can fire even during the northern wet months when Indian Ocean storm systems track in a different pattern. G-Land similarly can have good surf outside the main window. And some north-facing breaks on Sumatra and the northern Mentawais can produce surf during the wet season when northwest swells wrap in. But for a first-time Indonesia trip, May through October is the answer.

Getting There

The Hub: Bali (Ngurah Rai International Airport, DPS)

Almost every international surf trip to Indonesia begins and ends in Bali. Ngurah Rai International Airport in Denpasar receives direct or one-stop flights from most major cities worldwide, and it sits at the centre of the regional network that connects to Lombok, Sumbawa, Java, and Sumatra. Even if your final destination is the Mentawais or G-Land, you will almost certainly route through Bali.

Airlines and Routes

From North America: The most common routings are via Tokyo, Seoul, Hong Kong, or Singapore with a single connection to Denpasar. Singapore Airlines (via Singapore/SIN) and Cathay Pacific (via Hong Kong/HKG) consistently receive strong reviews for service, reliability, and handling of surf luggage on this route. Korean Air and Japan Airlines via their respective hubs are solid alternatives. Direct flights from North America to Bali do not exist; a transit in Asia is always required, adding roughly 20–24 hours of total travel time from the US West Coast.

From Australia: Bali is practically a domestic destination from an Australian perspective. Qantas, Virgin Australia, and Jetstar all fly direct from Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, and Perth. Flight time from Perth is approximately 3.5 hours; from the east coast, around 6 hours. Australians dominate the Bali surf lineup for good reason — it is one of the most accessible international surf destinations on the planet from the east coast of Australia.

From Europe: Emirates (via Dubai), Singapore Airlines (via Singapore), and Cathay Pacific (via Hong Kong) are the primary options. Expect 18–22 hours total travel time from Western Europe.

Surfboards

Surfboard fees and policies vary widely by carrier and are worth researching specifically for your chosen airline before booking. Most carriers charge an oversized or sports equipment fee; budget 50–100 USD per direction as a baseline. A well-padded board bag that fits two or three boards is standard — single-board bags draw the same fees and offer less protection per board. Hard-shell board coffins offer maximum protection but attract attention at check-in. Wrap fins and leash plugs carefully and ship boards without fins installed.

Within Indonesia, domestic connections — Bali to Lombok, Bali to Sumbawa, Bali to Padang for the Mentawais — are served primarily by Garuda Indonesia, Citilink, and Lion Air. All three carry boards with varying fees; Garuda is generally the most reliable and predictable for oversized luggage. Book early on domestic sectors as board fees can be inconsistently applied depending on check-in staff.

The Regions and Spots

Bali — The Bukit Peninsula

The limestone plateau at the southern tip of Bali holds the highest concentration of quality reef breaks in Indonesia. Most are left-handers that break along the base of the peninsula's cliffs, oriented to receive Indian Ocean swell from the south and southwest.

Uluwatu is the iconic centrepiece — a left-hand reef break that wraps around the base of a 70-metre cliff, accessed through a limestone cave. The wave has multiple sections: the Racetrack is a long, open-face wall ideal for carving; Temples is a hollower, more critical section further inside; the Peak takes the most southwest-angled swells and is the crowd favourite. On a 1.5–2.5m (5–8ft) Indian Ocean swell with morning offshore winds, Uluwatu is as good as any wave in the world. It is also among the most crowded. Arrive at dawn.

Best season: May through October. Offshore winds are most reliable in the early morning, typically glassing off by 10–11am as the sea breeze builds.

Padang Padang sits in a small cove a few kilometres from Uluwatu and is an entirely different proposition. It is a short, extremely hollow left-hand barrel that breaks over a very shallow reef and only switches on when a solid swell — minimum 1.5m (5ft), ideally 2m+ (6ft+) — pushes through the cove entrance. When it's on, it is one of the most intense barrels in Bali. When it's too small, it's a forgettable closeout. The Billabong Pro Padang Padang was held here for years. Not a beginner wave under any circumstances.

Best season: June through September on the biggest Indian Ocean pulses.

Keramas on Bali's east coast is the island's best right-hander — a fast, hollow reef break that fires on south swells and benefits from cleaner conditions than the more exposed Bukit spots. It's less crowded than Uluwatu or Padang Padang and holds size well. The WSL has held events here under floodlights.

Best season: May through October, particularly when south swells have a slight southeast angle.

Lombok — Desert Point

A three-hour fast-boat crossing from Bali (or a short flight) brings you to Lombok, a larger and significantly less developed island that holds one of the most revered left-hand barrels in the world.

Desert Point (Bangko Bangko) sits at the remote southwest tip of Lombok, an hour's drive beyond the end of the paved road. The wave is a grinding, hollow left-hander that breaks over a flat, shallow reef and on a good day peels for 200 metres or more without backing off. It is fickle and demanding — it can be flat for four days and then deliver a week of perfect, ankle-deep tubes — and the logistics (limited accommodation, dusty road, no real infrastructure) filter out the casual visitor. For surfers willing to wait, it is among the best waves anywhere.

Lombok is predominantly Muslim, distinct from Hindu Bali in culture and atmosphere. Dress modestly in town and be respectful of prayer times.

Best season: June through August on solid south/southwest swells. The wave needs a minimum threshold of swell to work properly — under about 1.2m (4ft) it's unsurfable; above 2m (6ft) it becomes one of the most incredible experiences in surfing.

East Java — G-Land (Grajagan)

G-Land is one of the legends. Located inside the Alas Purwo National Park at the southeastern tip of Java, it is a long left-hand point break with multiple sections — Kongs, Speedie's, Money Trees — that can link up on a good swell for rides of over 500 metres. The wave is powerful, fast, and unforgiving. It has hosted WSL Championship Tour events and has a reputation that has attracted every generation of progressive surfers since it was first surfed in the early 1970s.

Access is by boat from Bali (a 4–5 hour crossing across the Bali Strait) or via a combination of flight and road through Banyuwangi in East Java. The surf camps — Bobby's, Joyo's, and G-Land Surf Camp — must be booked in advance; they are the only accommodation option and control access to the break. Costs are higher than standard Bali travel but include accommodation, food, and boat transfers.

The jungle setting is dramatic: the camps sit directly behind the beach in dense rainforest, and monkeys and hornbills are regular companions. Cell service is limited.

Best season: May through October, with June and July typically producing the most consistent and powerful conditions.

The Mentawai Islands

If one place on earth can legitimately claim to have the highest concentration of world-class surf breaks, it is the Mentawai Islands — a chain of islands off the west coast of Sumatra, roughly 150 kilometres offshore. The islands face the open Indian Ocean directly and receive consistent long-period swells for most of the year. Within a relatively small area there are dozens of named breaks of genuine world-class quality.

The best-known include HT's (Hollow Trees / Lance's Right) — a perfect, mechanically consistent right-hand barrel that many professional surfers consider the single best wave in the world on its day; Macaronis, a fun, long, forgiving right-hander with a workable barrel section; Rifles, a left-hand barrel; Bank Vaults, a heavy left-hand slab; and Lance's Left, a powerful, barreling left opposite HT's.

Access to the Mentawais is primarily by live-aboard charter boat from Padang, Sumatra (a 30-minute flight from Bali, then an 8–12 hour boat crossing). This is the standard approach — trips run 7 to 14 days, boats carry 6–12 surfers, and the ability to move between breaks based on conditions is the main advantage. A handful of land-based resorts also operate on the islands, offering an alternative to the boat experience.

The Mentawais require more planning and budget than a standard Bali trip. A quality live-aboard charter runs significantly more than a week's surfing in Bali. But for intermediate to advanced surfers chasing the best waves of their lives, the trip is in a different category.

Best season: April through October, with May through August consistently producing the best and most sustained swell.

Nias — Lagundri Bay

Nias is a large island off the west coast of North Sumatra, well north of the Mentawais. Lagundri Bay on the island's south coast holds a right-hand point break that was one of the first Indonesian waves to achieve global fame — the photos of empty, perfect rights in the late 1970s helped define the era of surf exploration. A major earthquake in 2005 raised the reef by approximately one metre and many surfers believe it actually improved the wave, adding more punch and hollowness to the take-off.

The wave itself is a consistent, powerful right-hander that peels along a coral point with good length and a barreling section near the end. Lagundri is considerably less visited than the Mentawais or Bali and offers a more genuine off-the-beaten-path experience.

Access is by flight from Medan (North Sumatra) or from Singapore. The infrastructure is basic. Lagundri village is welcoming to surfers but this is not a polished tourist destination.

Best season: June through October on Indian Ocean south swells.

Culture, Language, and People

Bali: Hindu Island in a Muslim Archipelago

Bali's culture is a living expression of a distinct form of Hinduism that has developed on the island for over a thousand years. Temples are everywhere — from towering sea temples like Tanah Lot and Uluwatu's famous cliff-top pura, to tiny household shrines outside every family compound. Every morning, Balinese women place canang sari — small woven palm offerings of flowers and incense — at doorways, on the street, and at the base of sacred trees. These offerings are not decorative; they are devotional acts, and walking on them or treating them carelessly is genuinely disrespectful.

Away from the beach, dress modestly. A sarong and sash are required to enter any Balinese temple — these are almost always available to borrow or rent at the entrance. Shorts and a singlet are fine at the beach and in surf areas; not fine in a village ceremony or a family compound.

The Balinese are patient and warm with visitors who make even minimal effort to engage respectfully. A smile, a greeting in Indonesian, and patience when things don't move on Western time will take you a long way.

Bahasa Indonesia

Indonesia's national language, Bahasa Indonesia, is one of the most accessible Asian languages for English speakers. The grammar has no tenses in the Western sense, no gendered nouns, and relatively consistent pronunciation. A handful of phrases will improve every interaction:

  • Selamat pagi — Good morning
  • Selamat siang — Good afternoon
  • Selamat malam — Good evening
  • Terima kasih — Thank you
  • Sama-sama — You're welcome
  • Berapa harga? — How much?
  • Maaf — Sorry / Excuse me
  • Ya / Tidak — Yes / No
  • Tolong — Please (when asking for help)
  • Enak sekali — Delicious (use this often)

On Bali, many people also speak Balinese at home — a distinct language from Bahasa Indonesia. In the Mentawais and on Nias, local languages predominate in villages, though Bahasa Indonesia functions as the lingua franca.

Surf Etiquette and Local Respect

Indonesia's most famous breaks have established local surf communities who have surfed these waves their entire lives. At Uluwatu, local surfers have clear priority in the lineup and the social hierarchy is real. Paddling into the set wave over a local who has been sitting in position is not a cultural misunderstanding — it's a universal breach of surf etiquette that will be received accordingly.

At more remote breaks — desert Point, G-Land, parts of the Mentawais — local fishing communities have a relationship with the land and water that predates surf tourism by generations. Buy locally, be visible in a positive way, and understand that the privilege of surfing empty world-class waves in someone's community is not owed to you.

What to Bring

Boards

Bring at least two boards. Ding repair in Bali is widely available and good quality — there are experienced shapers and repair shops in Kuta, Canggu, and Uluwatu — but waiting two days for a ding repair in pumping 2-metre surf is its own kind of suffering. For a standard Bali trip: a shortboard for solid days at Uluwatu or Padang Padang, and a slightly longer, more versatile board for everyday conditions. If you're heading to the Mentawais or Desert Point, step up the thickness and volume — the waves are powerful and paddling into them requires more than a competition-thin board.

Reef booties are not standard in Bali but are worth packing if you plan to surf shallow spots. A full set of spare fins, a leash per board, and surf wax (Bali sells wax but tropical wax only — bring your own if you run warm).

Health and Medical

Recommended vaccinations before travel: Hepatitis A, Hepatitis B, Typhoid, and ensure Tetanus is current. Consult a travel medicine clinic 4–6 weeks before departure.

Malaria is not a concern in Bali. It is a concern on some of the outer islands — parts of Sumbawa, remote Sumatra, and some Mentawai islands — so if you're heading beyond Bali, check the current status for your specific destination and consult a doctor about prophylaxis.

Dengue fever is present throughout Indonesia. It's mosquito-transmitted and there is no reliable prophylactic — the only protection is avoiding bites. Use repellent (DEET-based is most effective), cover up at dusk, and sleep under a mosquito net if your accommodation doesn't have screened windows or air conditioning.

Bali Belly — the gastrointestinal misery that arrives from unfamiliar food or water — is a rite of passage that most first-time visitors experience to some degree. Drink bottled or filtered water only. Be cautious with ice in remote areas. Street food is generally safe at busy, high-turnover warung stalls; less so at quiet roadside spots. Carry rehydration salts (ORS sachets) and a basic antimotility medication; your body will likely adapt after a few days.

Reef cuts from coral are common and can become infected very quickly in tropical conditions. Clean any reef cut immediately and thoroughly with fresh water, apply antiseptic, and monitor closely. A minor cut that looks trivial on day one can be a significant infection by day three in the tropics.

Rabies is present in Bali — primarily in dogs and monkeys. The monkeys at Uluwatu Temple are infamously bold and will steal anything they can grab. Do not feed them, do not make sustained eye contact with them, and keep sunglasses on your face rather than your head. A monkey bite requires immediate medical attention.

Practical Items

  • Travel insurance with medical evacuation coverage is non-negotiable, particularly for remote areas
  • Cash (Indonesian Rupiah) — ATMs are plentiful in tourist Bali but scarce in the Mentawais, at Desert Point, and at G-Land; carry enough before you leave the main infrastructure
  • Reef-safe sunscreen — Indonesia has introduced restrictions on certain chemical sunscreens, and protecting the reef is a genuine local concern
  • A quality dry bag or waterproof case for your phone and valuables
  • Power bank — consistent electricity can be unreliable in remote areas
  • A basic first aid kit: antiseptic, bandages, reef cut treatment, ORS sachets, antihistamine, anti-diarrheal medication, and any prescription medications you take regularly
  • Sarong — essential for temple visits, useful as a beach towel, a shade layer, and a light blanket

Visa and Entry

Most nationalities, including US, Australian, UK, EU, and Canadian passport holders, are eligible for a Visa on Arrival (VOA) at Ngurah Rai International Airport in Bali. The current fee is approximately USD 35 for a 30-day stay, extendable once for an additional 30 days (total 60 days). You will need your passport, proof of onward travel, and the fee in cash or by card.

Some nationalities must obtain a visa in advance through the Indonesian embassy. Check the current Indonesian immigration rules for your specific passport well before travel, as policies have changed in recent years.

When to Go

For Bali and the Bukit: June through August hits the peak. Consistent south and southwest swells, reliable morning offshores at Uluwatu and Padang Padang, and warm water. It is also the busiest and most expensive period. May and September are excellent compromise months — strong surf, slightly less crowded lineups.

For Desert Point (Lombok): June through August is the window. The wave needs south/southwest swell energy that is most consistent in the heart of the Indian Ocean swell season. Plan to stay at least a week to increase your chances of catching it when it switches on.

For G-Land: May through July before the trade winds become too strong. G-Land can handle size but becomes messy in very strong onshore or cross-shore conditions; the early dry season window often produces the cleanest days.

For the Mentawais: A 10–14 day live-aboard trip scheduled for May, June, or September balances swell consistency with slightly fewer boats in the water than the July–August peak. The Mentawais are never empty but July and August attract the most charter traffic.

For Nias: June through October. The wave doesn't require the same swell window precision as Desert Point — it can work on a wider range of conditions — but the peak Indian Ocean months produce the best and most powerful surf.
