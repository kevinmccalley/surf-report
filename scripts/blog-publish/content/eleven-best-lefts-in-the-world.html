The Left-Hander

A left-hand wave breaks from left to right as you look at it from the beach — the surfer rides toward their left. For goofy-footers (right foot forward), it is a frontside ride: the wave face opens up in full view, every turn made facing the lip. For regular-footers, it is a backhand challenge: vision and body position require more work, which is why many of the world's most respected backhand surfers have built their reputations at the world's greatest lefts.

Some of the most celebrated waves in the history of surfing break left. These eleven are among the finest.

1. Teahupo'o — Tahiti, French Polynesia

Teahupo'o is the heaviest wave on earth that is regularly surfed. Located on the south coast of Tahiti in French Polynesia, it breaks over an extremely shallow coral reef that rises abruptly from deep water, causing incoming south swells to stand up almost vertically and throw a thick, wide, cylindrical barrel with tremendous force. The lip of a large Teahupo'o wave is as thick as it is tall — the wave does not so much barrel as detonate.

The WSL holds a Championship Tour event here, and footage of the world's best surfers inside a Teahupo'o barrel — compressed into a space that looks physically impossible — has defined modern surfing. At large size (overhead and above), the wave is considered one of the most dangerous in professional surfing. The surrounding landscape — jungle-covered volcanic peaks dropping to a turquoise lagoon — makes it one of the most beautiful surf environments on earth.

Best season: May through October on south and southwest swells generated by Southern Ocean storm systems. Water temperature: 27–29°C (81–84°F) — boardshorts.

2. Pipeline (Banzai Pipeline) — North Shore, Oahu, Hawaii

Pipeline breaks left. This is worth stating clearly because the wave is so central to the mythology of surfing that its direction is sometimes confused with Backdoor, the right-hand barrel that breaks off the same peak. Pipeline is the left, and it is the wave that defined tube riding as surfing's ultimate act.

The wave breaks over a two-tiered lava reef on the North Shore of Oahu. It stands up quickly and throws a large, round barrel with enough volume to ride inside, enough depth to survive an error, and enough force to kill if the error is bad enough. The most critical section is the take-off: the lip throws early, the reef is immediately beneath, and the barrel moves faster than it looks from the channel. The WSL Pipeline Pro is surfing's most prestigious event.

Best season: November through February on northwest and north groundswells from the North Pacific storm track. Swell period is critical — 14+ seconds produces the best barrel shape. Water temperature: 24–26°C (75–79°F).

3. G-Land (Grajagan) — East Java, Indonesia

G-Land is one of the great left-hand point breaks — a long, powerful, multi-section wave that breaks along the edge of the Alas Purwo National Park at the southeastern tip of Java. The wave has three primary sections — Kongs, Speedie's, and Money Trees — that on a significant south swell link together into rides of 500 metres or more, with barrel sections, fast open walls, and enough power to demand serious surfing skill throughout.

Access by boat from Bali (a 4–5 hour crossing of the Bali Strait) or overland through Banyuwangi in East Java. The surf camps — which must be booked in advance and are the only accommodation option — sit directly behind the beach in dense rainforest. Monkeys, hornbills, and the sound of the jungle at night are part of the experience. G-Land is not a place you visit casually; the logistics ensure it remains reserved for those who make the effort.

Best season: May through October on Indian Ocean south swells. Best: June and July on long-period groundswell of 1.5m (5ft) or more.

4. Desert Point — Lombok, Indonesia

Desert Point, at the remote southwest tip of Lombok, produces a grinding, hollow left-hand barrel that on a good day peels for 200 metres or more over a flat, shallow reef with almost mechanical consistency. The wave stands up on the inside section, throws a wide lip, and the barrel runs — and keeps running — along the reef at a speed that demands both commitment and tube-reading ability.

The wave is fickle. Desert Point can be flat for days at a time, then switch on suddenly when the swell period and direction align. The surrounding infrastructure is minimal: basic accommodation, no real town, a dusty road, and a population of serious surfers who have come specifically to wait for the wave. That willingness to wait — and to be rewarded with one of the greatest left-hand barrels in the world — is the point.

Best season: June through August on solid Indian Ocean south and southwest swells. The wave needs a minimum swell threshold to break properly — under about 1.2m (4ft) it closes out; above 1.8m (6ft) it becomes transcendent.

5. Mundaka — Basque Country, Spain

Mundaka is the jewel of European surfing — a left-hand river mouth break in the Basque Country of northern Spain that, on a well-aimed northwest Atlantic swell, produces hollow, barreling lefts of exceptional quality. The wave is formed by the Oka River estuary depositing sand at its mouth into the Bay of Biscay; the resulting sandbar creates a perfectly shaped left-hander that bears more resemblance to an Indonesian point break than anything else on the Atlantic coast.

The catch: the sandbar is fickle. Dredging operations in the estuary have periodically destroyed the wave's consistency. When the sand is right and a solid northwest groundswell arrives with light east winds, Mundaka fires at a level that matches anywhere in the world. When the sand is wrong, it's an average beach break. The WSL has held events here that have produced some of the best surfing ever seen in Europe. The surrounding Basque culture — pintxos, txakoli wine, the old fishing town of Mundaka itself — makes it one of the most civilised surf destinations on the planet.

Best season: October through March on northwest Atlantic groundswells. Offshore winds blow from the east in autumn and winter.

6. Skeleton Bay (Donkergat) — Skeleton Coast, Namibia

Skeleton Bay is the longest left-hand wave in the world when it fires. Located on the Skeleton Coast of Namibia — one of the most remote and inhospitable coastlines on earth — it is a sand-bottom point break that on very specific conditions (long-period south swell, low tide, light southeast wind) peels along a kilometre or more of beach, producing rides that last four to seven minutes without interruption. Video of the wave emerged in the early 2010s and immediately recalibrated what surfers thought was possible in terms of wave length.

The context is extreme. The Skeleton Coast is a desert. The surrounding environment is ancient, barren, and entirely unforgiving. The nearest town is Swakopmund, several hours away. Access requires a 4WD vehicle, permits for the national park, and serious logistical preparation. Water temperature is cold year-round due to the Benguela Current upwelling from the South Atlantic — 14–18°C (57–64°F) — and a thick wetsuit is essential even in midsummer. Great white sharks are present in these waters.

Best season: April through September on large, long-period south swells. The wave requires very specific conditions and does not fire on every swell — it is an expedition destination for the dedicated.

7. Cloudbreak — Tavarua, Fiji

Cloudbreak is Fiji's most famous wave and one of the great left-hand reefs of the world. Located off the island of Tavarua in the Mamanuca archipelago, it breaks over a coral reef in deep water and produces a long, powerful left-hander that steps through several distinct sections — a heavy take-off zone, a barreling mid-section, and an open wall that allows radical surfing all the way to the channel.

At 2–3m (6–10ft) Cloudbreak is a world-class but approachable wave for skilled surfers. At 3m+ (10ft+) it becomes one of the more serious big-wave left-handers in the Pacific — powerful, hollow, and demanding of precision. The WSL held its historic Fiji Pro events here, producing some of the most memorable performances of the modern era. Access is by boat from Tavarua or Namotu islands.

Best season: May through October on south and southwest groundswells. The Fijian swell window is reliable and consistent through the Southern Ocean winter months.

8. Uluwatu — Bukit Peninsula, Bali, Indonesia

Uluwatu is the signature wave of Bali — a left-hand reef break that wraps along the base of the Bukit Peninsula's limestone cliffs, accessed through a cave in the cliff face, and produces one of the most consistent and varied left-handers in the world. The wave has multiple sections: the Racetrack is a long, fast wall ideal for progressive surfing; Temples is a hollower section further inside; the Peak is the most exposed and takes the biggest swell.

The setting is extraordinary — the ancient Hindu temple perched on the cliff above, the cave entrance to the break below, the turquoise Indian Ocean stretching to the horizon. On a 1.5–2.5m swell with morning offshore winds, Uluwatu is as good as any wave in Southeast Asia. It is also genuinely crowded from sunrise to sunset during the season. Arriving at dawn, before the crowd builds, produces the best experience.

Best season: May through October on Indian Ocean south and southwest swells. Morning offshore winds are most reliable in June through August.

9. Chicama — La Libertad, Peru

Chicama is one of the longest waves in the world. Located on the Peruvian coast north of Trujillo, it is a left-hand point break that on a proper southwest groundswell peels along a series of points — El Hombre, El Punto, and El Faro — for distances that can exceed two kilometres. A single ride at Chicama can last several minutes and cover more ground than a full session at most breaks.

The wave is not particularly hollow or heavy — it is defined by length, consistency, and the cold Humboldt Current upwelling that makes the Peruvian coast one of the most swell-consistent on the Pacific rim. Water temperature is cold year-round: 16–20°C (61–68°F), requiring a wetsuit even in summer. The town of Chicama is small and unpretentious; this is not a resort destination but a wave destination, and the surfers who come here are primarily here for the wave.

Best season: March through October on south and southwest swells. Best: April through August.

10. Pavones — Golfo Dulce, Costa Rica

Pavones sits in the Golfo Dulce on the Pacific coast of southern Costa Rica and is famous for one thing: length. On a large south or southwest swell, the left-hand point peels along a cobblestone and sand bottom for up to 800 metres, producing one of the longest left-hand rides in the Americas. The wave is powerful without being dangerously heavy, and it rewards both classical, arcing turns and hanging time in the barrel sections that develop on larger swells.

The surrounding jungle — howler monkeys, toucans, bullet ants the size of a thumb — and the remoteness of the location (the end of a long dirt road in one of the most sparsely populated corners of Costa Rica) add to the experience. The town of Pavones is small, community-oriented, and has been a destination for travelling surfers since the 1970s.

Best season: May through November on south and southwest swells. Peak: June through September. The best swells are long-period groundswells of 14+ seconds; shorter period swells produce a mediocre wave.

11. Shipsterns Bluff — Tasman Peninsula, Tasmania, Australia

Shipsterns Bluff — Shippies to those who surf it — is one of the most extreme left-hand waves in the world. Located on the remote Tasman Peninsula of southern Tasmania, it breaks over a shallow reef and produces a heavy, multi-stepped wave face that creates a series of ledges within the barrel itself. The steps — caused by the irregular reef geometry — make the drop unpredictable and the wipeouts violent. It is not a wave that rewards anything other than total commitment.

Access requires either a 2-hour hike through Tasmanian wilderness or a boat ride. There are no facilities, no crowds, and no infrastructure. The wave breaks in cold, shark-inhabited southern ocean water. A thick wetsuit and a high threshold for consequence are both non-negotiable. In return, Shipsterns offers one of the most raw, uncompromised surfing experiences available anywhere on earth.

Best season: May through August on large Southern Ocean groundswells. The wave needs significant size to expose its best sections — anything under 2m (6ft) is relatively tame.
