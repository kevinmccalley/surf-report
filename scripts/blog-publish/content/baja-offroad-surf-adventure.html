The Last Great Surf Road Trip

Drive south from the US border and the surf crowds thin fast. By the time you're an hour below Ensenada, you're sharing waves with local fishermen and the occasional pelican. Push deeper into the peninsula — past El Rosario where the paved world starts to feel tentative, through the cardón cactus desert, down to Scorpion Bay — and you'll find point breaks that peel for hundreds of metres with nobody on them.

Baja California is approximately 1,300 kilometres of Pacific coastline, most of it undeveloped. The peninsula runs roughly north to south, angled slightly to the northwest, with its Pacific face exposed to both the North Pacific storm track in winter and the South Pacific swell season in summer. That means there are good waves here for most of the year — but which spots fire, and when, depends entirely on swell direction.

This is not a trip for the unprepared. The rewards are proportional to the effort.

The Two Swell Windows

Northwest Swells (October – April)

Winter and early spring bring North Pacific low-pressure systems tracking across the Pacific and generating northwest swells that hit the Baja coast almost head-on. These are long-period swells — 13 to 18 seconds is common — and they energise the northern and central Baja points with considerable force. The best spots for northwest energy are the exposed points and reefs from Ensenada south to Punta Abreojos, plus the big-wave venue at Isla Todos Santos just offshore from Ensenada.

Water temperature in northern Baja during winter drops to around 14–16°C (57–61°F). A 4/3 wetsuit with booties is the standard kit from November through March.

South and Southwest Swells (May – October)

As the year moves into late spring and summer, the swell window shifts south. Storms tracking through the Southern Ocean generate south and southwest swells that travel north toward Baja. Unlike northern California — where the Channel Islands shadow many breaks from south energy — much of Baja's central and southern Pacific coast is fully exposed to this swell direction.

Scorpion Bay (San Juanico) is the headline act. Its south-facing bay receives these swells directly and the result is one of the longest and most consistent right-hand point break systems in the world. Water temperature in the south in summer reaches 22–26°C (72–79°F) — boardshorts territory.

The Spots

San Miguel — Northern Baja, ~40 minutes south of Tijuana

The easiest Baja point break to access and one of the best. San Miguel is a long right-hander that wraps around a rocky point just north of Ensenada, breaking over a cobblestone and reef bottom. On a solid northwest swell it produces walls of 150 metres or more, with a takeoff that rewards timing and a mid-section that can run for a long time before closing out near the rocks.

Best season: October through March on northwest swells in the 1.5–3m (5–10ft) range. The spot handles size well but gets crowded by Baja standards in the winter months. It's directly accessible from Highway 1 — no offroad required — making it a natural first stop or warm-up day before pushing further south.

Killers — Isla Todos Santos — Boat access from Ensenada

Technically not an offroad destination — you get there by boat from Ensenada, not by truck — but worth including because it's the most serious wave in northern Baja. Killers is a big-wave right-hander on the north side of Isla Todos Santos, an island sitting about 20 kilometres off the coast. The wave breaks over a deep-water reef and needs genuine size to work — it typically requires a northwest swell of at least 3–4m (10–12ft) face height before it shows its character, and at 5–6m (16–20ft) it becomes one of the most impressive waves in the North Pacific.

Best season: December through February on the largest northwest swells of the year. Pangas and panga tours depart from Ensenada when conditions warrant. Not a beginner or intermediate wave under any circumstances.

Punta Baja — Remote point, ~120km south of El Rosario

Punta Baja is where the trip gets serious. Located well below El Rosario — the last real town before the central Baja desert — the point requires a dirt-road detour off Highway 1 and sits in a small fishing community at the edge of the peninsula. The wave is a right-hand point break that works on northwest swells, producing powerful, hollow sections over a mixed rock and sand bottom.

The area is extremely remote. Cell service is non-existent. The fishing village has no tourist infrastructure. Bring everything you need — fuel, water, food, spare parts — and plan to be entirely self-sufficient. The reward is a legitimately uncrowded point break in a dramatic, untouched setting.

Best season: November through March on northwest swells. Best at low to mid tide.

Punta Abreojos — Central Baja Pacific coast, ~260km south of El Rosario

Abreojos is one of the jewels of central Baja. The road from Highway 1 has been largely improved but the drive remains remote — plan for approximately 60 kilometres off the main highway through open desert. The town itself is a lobster fishing community, and the relationship between visiting surfers and locals is built on mutual respect: be courteous, buy supplies locally, and don't assume access is guaranteed.

The wave is a powerful right-hand point break that wraps around the headland and produces fast, hollow sections on northwest swells. It can also fire on southwest swell energy when the angle is right. The surrounding area holds multiple additional breaks accessible only by exploring on foot or in a high-clearance vehicle.

Best season: October through April on northwest and southwest swells. The point needs at least 1.5m (5ft) of energy to link up properly. Bring a step-up board — the wave can get heavy.

Scorpion Bay (San Juanico) — Bahia San Juanico, central Baja Pacific coast

No spot on the peninsula has captured the imagination of travelling surfers more than Scorpion Bay. San Juanico sits inside a southwest-facing bay on the central Baja coast, and when a long-period south or southwest swell rolls in, the points step down the bay in sequence — up to seven distinct peaks, each one handing off to the next — producing rides that can last three to four minutes on an ideal day.

The wave itself is a right-hander with a mellow, forgiving face and excellent length. It rewards nose-riding and classic longboard surfing as much as shortboarding. The town of San Juanico is small and welcoming, with a handful of palapa camps and basic amenities. Most surfers camp on the beach for days or weeks at a time.

The road from Highway 1 is partially paved and partially dirt, approximately 60 kilometres each way. A standard 4WD vehicle with decent clearance handles it without trouble in dry conditions; in wet weather it becomes considerably more demanding. The drive is part of the experience.

Best season: May through October on south and southwest swells. Peak is June through September when Southern Ocean activity is highest. The bay is sheltered enough that it needs a proper long-period south swell (14 seconds or longer) to really link up — a short-period south windchop won't do much here. Check swell period carefully before making the drive.

Los Cerritos — Near Todos Santos, Baja California Sur

At the southern end of the peninsula, Los Cerritos is a wide, gently curved beach break that handles south swells from a direction that hits it cleanly. The wave is significantly mellower than anything in central Baja — it's a rolling beach break rather than a point — but it's consistent, forgiving, and one of the best beginner and intermediate waves in the entire peninsula. The beach is wide and sandy with good facilities relative to most Baja surf spots.

Best season: May through October on south swells. Summer south swells produce the best-shaped, most consistent conditions. The spot is accessible from the town of Todos Santos, which has comfortable accommodation, good food, and a functioning tourist infrastructure that makes it a useful base for exploring the southern cape region.

What to Bring

The Vehicle

A capable 4WD truck or SUV with genuine high clearance is the right tool for any serious Baja surf mission. Two full-size spare tyres is the minimum — rough volcanic rock on dirt tracks destroys sidewalls without warning. Carry at least 20 additional litres of fuel beyond what your tank holds; the gaps between fuel stations in central Baja are longer than most visitors expect. A recovery kit (traction boards, tow strap, hi-lift jack, shovel) is essential if you plan to leave paved roads.

Water and Food

Plan for at least 10 litres of drinking water per person per day in central and southern Baja during summer — the desert heat is not negotiable. A proper filter (Sawyer or LifeStraw) and purification tablets are good backup. Buy food in Ensenada before dropping south: the further you go, the more limited supplies become, though coastal fishing villages often sell fresh fish and lobster directly.

Surf Gear

Bring more boards than you think you need. A broken board in central Baja means a broken trip. For a typical northwest swell mission: a shortboard for punchy conditions, a mid-length for more lined-up points, and something with float for smaller days. For a Scorpion Bay south swell trip: add a longboard or noserider — the wave rewards length.

Reef booties are worth carrying even in warm water. Central Baja points break over volcanic rock and urchins are common. A full repair kit (resin, cloth, sandpaper, ding tape for emergency patches) is essential.

Water temperatures vary significantly by location and season:

  • Northern Baja, winter (Oct–Mar): 14–17°C (57–63°F) — 4/3 wetsuit, consider booties and hood
  • Central/southern Baja, summer (Jun–Sep): 22–27°C (72–81°F) — boardshorts or 2/2 spring suit

Paperwork and Money

Mexican auto insurance is mandatory — US and Canadian policies do not cover you south of the border and driving uninsured is a serious risk. Buy it online before you leave; it's inexpensive. A tourist card (Forma Migratoria Múltiple, or FMM) is required for travel beyond the Baja California Free Zone (typically anything south of about 30km from the border). Carry your passport.

Cash (Mexican pesos) is essential beyond Ensenada. Most fishing villages operate entirely on cash. ATMs exist in towns like Guerrero Negro, Santa Rosalía, Loreto, and La Paz, but not in the surf zones.

Communication and Safety

A satellite communicator — Garmin inReach or SPOT device — is strongly recommended for travel to remote spots like Punta Baja and Abreojos. Cell coverage disappears well south of Ensenada on most carriers, and a vehicle breakdown or medical emergency in the central Baja desert with no way to communicate is a serious situation. Leave a detailed itinerary — including planned campsites and check-in dates — with someone at home before departing.

Do not drive at night. Cattle roam freely on Baja roads and highway markings are inconsistent. Most experienced Baja travellers consider a strict no-night-driving rule non-negotiable.

When to Go

October – November is the ideal window for a northern and central Baja NW swell trip. The desert heat has broken, the water in the north is still manageable without a heavy suit, and the first serious northwest swells of the season arrive. San Miguel, Punta Baja, and Abreojos are all worth targeting.

December – February brings the most powerful northwest swell energy of the year. Killers on Todos Santos can wake up. The northern points see their best surf. Pack a proper wetsuit.

June – September is Scorpion Bay season. Time your trip around a south swell pulse — check long-range forecasts for 14-second-plus south energy out of the Southern Ocean and plan to be at San Juanico when it arrives. Los Cerritos and the southern cape are also at their best.

March – May and late October are transition periods between the swell windows — less predictable, but the desert landscape and empty roads have their own appeal.

Respecting What You Find

Baja's surf spots exist within fishing communities that have been there for generations. The unwritten rule that governs most of these places is straightforward: the fishing families hold priority, locals come second, and visiting surfers earn their place through behaviour, not by showing up with a truck full of gear. Buy your supplies locally. Ask before camping on private land. Don't honk or crowd a lineup that was empty before you arrived.

The Baja that rewards patience and humility is the Baja worth finding.
